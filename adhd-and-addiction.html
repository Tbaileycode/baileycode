ADHD and addiction: the link, explained.

Why people with ADHD are more likely to struggle with addiction, how undiagnosed ADHD can make recovery harder, and what to do about it. Plain language, from lived experience.

The short answer: yes, ADHD and addiction are linked. People with ADHD are more likely to develop substance use problems, and ADHD often goes unrecognised in people in recovery. Understanding the link tends to replace shame with a plan.

Why the link exists

  • Reward and dopamine. ADHD brains tend to chase stimulation and reward. Substances, gambling, scrolling and other compulsive habits deliver it fast.
  • Impulsivity. Acting before the thought has finished makes it harder to stop a habit from forming.
  • Self-medication. Many people find a substance calms racing thoughts, helps them focus or lets them feel in control, at least at first.
  • Emotional intensity. Big feelings, rejection sensitivity and shame can drive people toward whatever numbs them.
  • Trauma and ADHD overlap. Both are common, and each can make the other harder to see and treat.

Why it matters in recovery

If you are in recovery and keep struggling with overwhelm, not knowing where to start, losing track of time, or being flattened by one slip, it can feel like a personal failing. Sometimes it is ADHD showing up. That does not make it an excuse. It makes it something with a name and with workable strategies.

This lens is useful even without an ADHD diagnosis. Many of the same patterns show up broadly in recovery, and the same tools help.

Is it ADHD, addiction, or both?

Substance use and withdrawal can look like ADHD, and ADHD can drive substance use, so it is genuinely hard to untangle on your own. A qualified clinician, usually a psychiatrist, assesses your full history and decides. If you suspect ADHD, start with your GP. Be honest about your substance use history; it helps them, it does not count against you.

Common questions

Are ADHD and addiction linked?

Yes. Research consistently finds that people with ADHD are more likely to develop substance use problems than people without ADHD, and ADHD is common among people seeking addiction treatment.

Does ADHD cause addiction?

ADHD does not cause addiction by itself, but it raises the risk. Impulsivity, reward and dopamine differences, emotional intensity and years of unmanaged symptoms can all make substances, and other compulsive behaviours, more appealing.

Why do people with ADHD use substances?

Often to cope. Some people find a substance quiets a restless mind, helps them focus or lifts a flat mood, which is sometimes called self-medication. It works in the short term and costs a lot over time.

Can undiagnosed ADHD make recovery harder?

It can. Disorganisation, boredom, impulsivity and emotional overwhelm are easy to read as lack of willpower or commitment, when they may be untreated ADHD. Naming it can reduce shame and open up better support.

Is it ADHD or is it addiction?

They share symptoms such as poor concentration, restlessness and memory problems, and substance use and withdrawal can mimic ADHD. Only a qualified clinician can tell them apart, usually by looking at your whole history.

Does ADHD medication increase the risk of addiction?

This is a question for your prescriber. Treating ADHD is generally associated with better outcomes, but anyone with a history of substance use needs a careful, individual plan with their doctor.

Where can I get help?

Start with your GP, and ask about assessment by a psychiatrist who works with ADHD and substance use. In a crisis, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or 000 in an emergency.
