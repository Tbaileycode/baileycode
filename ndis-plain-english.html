NDIS terms in plain English.

The NDIS terms you will see in plans, reports and service agreements, explained simply. For participants, families, and support workers.

Why this page exists: NDIS plans, reports and service agreements are full of jargon. This is a plain-English guide to the terms you will see most. General information only; check ndis.gov.au for current rules.

NDIS terms, in plain English

Participant
A person with an NDIS plan.
Plan
The document that sets out a participant's goals and the funded supports they can use, and for how long.
Reasonable and necessary
The NDIS test for funding. A support must relate to the person's disability, be effective, good value for money and reasonable to fund.
Core supports
Funding for help with everyday life, such as personal care, household tasks, transport and community participation.
Capacity building supports
Funding to build skills and independence. Includes therapy, support coordination and employment support.
Capital supports
Funding for bigger items such as assistive technology, home modifications and specialist disability accommodation.
Support coordination
A capacity building support where a coordinator helps you understand your plan, find providers and put supports in place.
Provider
An individual or organisation that delivers supports. Providers can be registered with the NDIS or unregistered.
Agency-managed, plan-managed and self-managed
Three ways to manage plan funds. Agency-managed: the NDIA pays registered providers. Plan-managed: a plan manager pays your providers. Self-managed: you pay your providers yourself.
Service agreement
An agreement between a participant and a provider about what supports will be delivered, the price, and how changes and cancellations work.
Plan review
A scheduled check-in on how the plan is working, and what the next plan should include.
Functional capacity assessment
A report, often by an occupational therapist or other allied health professional, describing how a person's disability affects daily life. Often called an OT report.
Behaviour support plan
A plan written by a behaviour support practitioner to support positive behaviour and reduce the use of restrictive practices.
Supported Independent Living (SIL)
Help and supervision with daily living tasks for people who live in shared or individual homes and need ongoing support.

How to read an NDIS report without panicking

  1. Start with the recommendations. Jump to the end first: what is being asked for, and why.
  2. Read the "functional impact" parts as a description, not a verdict. They are written to show what support is needed, so they focus on difficulty.
  3. Circle every term you do not know and check it against the glossary above.
  4. Bring questions to your support coordinator or provider. It is their job to explain it.

Common questions

What does 'reasonable and necessary' mean in the NDIS?

It is the test the NDIS uses to decide whether to fund a support. The support must relate to your disability, be effective, offer value for money and be reasonable to fund.

What is the difference between core supports and capacity building?

Core supports help with everyday activities. Capacity building supports help you build skills and independence, such as therapy or support coordination.

What is the difference between plan-managed and self-managed?

With plan management, a plan manager pays your providers for you. With self-management, you pay your providers directly and keep the records yourself.

What is an OT report in the NDIS?

An occupational therapy report, often a functional capacity assessment, describing how your disability affects daily life and what supports might help. It is commonly used as evidence in planning and reviews.

Where can I check the current NDIS rules?

The NDIS website at ndis.gov.au is the source of truth. Rules and processes change, so confirm current details there or with your support coordinator.
